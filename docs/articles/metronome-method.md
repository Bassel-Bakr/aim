  tick as a reference rather than an order to click.
---

!!! info "Written by Bassel Bakr"
    Written from my own training and experience, not assembled from published sources. Treat it
    as one informed opinion rather than settled fact.

A metronome takes the decision of *when* to shoot away from you. Everything below is how to use that
without wasting a week.

- **Measure your own pace.** Targets divided by seconds, times 60. Start there, not at a guess.
- **Let accuracy pick the tempo.** Over 90% raise the beat, over 80% stay and work, under 80% lower
  it.
- **The beat is a reference, not necessarily a trigger.** Sometimes one target per beat, sometimes
  three.
- **It teaches you to budget tension.** The tick gives you no time to fix an overshoot.
- **Check it unpaced.** The run without a beat is the one that counts.

## What the beat is cutting

Take one shot apart. A flick, a moment confirming the crosshair is on the target, a trigger pull.

When the shot is bad, micro-corrections wedge themselves in. Micro, confirm, micro again.

None of it feels like time passing. **All of it is.**

Set the beat near the first bar and the second has nowhere to go. **You land the flick or you
miss.**

## Find your own pace

Play the scenario you actually train, once, at normal effort. Note the run length and the targets you
shot.

```
shots per second  =  targets / seconds
starting BPM      =  shots per second x 60
```

Sixty seconds and 92 targets is 1.53 shots per second. About 92 BPM.

Run it twice more if those disagree by more than a few percent. **A pace you cannot reproduce is
not a pace.**

## Set the beat by accuracy

Start at the number you measured. Accuracy tells you where to go next.

Read the paced run and do what its accuracy says.

**Over 90%:** the beat is still inside what you can do. Push it up, 5 or 10 BPM, and go again.

**Over 80%:** this is the tempo worth working on. Stay there until 90 comes back, then climb again.

**Under 80%:** the beat is ahead of your aim. Lower it until you are back in the band above.

<figcaption>Accuracy against tempo, with what to do at each level. The tempos that land you between
80 and 90 percent are the ones worth working on.</figcaption>
</figure>

There is a difference between a tempo you nearly hold and one far above it. The first teaches. The
second only trains missing on time, and your accuracy says so inside one run.

## Run it in blocks

Here is a shape to start from, not a rule. A block is a handful of runs with the metronome on, then
one with it off.

Train a whole session to a beat if you want to. The part worth keeping either way is the unpaced run,
because that is the one that tells you whether any of it reached your aim.

<div class="aim-steps aim-steps--tight" markdown>

1. 3 runs with the beat.
2. A run without it.
3. Read the paced accuracy: over 90% raise the beat, over 80% stay, under 80% lower it.
4. Read the unpaced run, held to your normal standards.
5. If the unpaced run got worse, measure your pace again and reset the beat from it.

</div>

A worse unpaced run usually means the tempo drifted away from what you can hold. Re-measure and set
the beat from the new number, the same way you found the first one.

## The beat is a reference, not necessarily a trigger { .aim-key-title }

!!! key ""
    The tick is not an order to click. It measures how fast you are going, and what you do with any
    one beat depends on what is in front of you.

On spread targets, one shot per beat is an honest rate.

In a cluster, one beat is enough for two or three targets. **Take them.**

On a flick across the screen, one target can cost two beats. **That is not a failure either.**

**Learning which of those you are in is the skill.** The metronome only makes your pace audible while you
learn it.

Do not chase the tick itself. Landing slightly before or after a beat changes nothing; drifting
further behind every beat is what matters.

## Clusters and long flicks

Static scenarios do not space targets evenly. That is where one-shot-per-beat falls apart.

Give a cluster the same beat you give the far target and you spend the cluster waiting. Give the far
target one beat and the tick arrives before your crosshair.

## Budgeting tension

This is what I think the tool actually trains.

A fixed beat leaves no time to repair an overshoot. Grip too hard, the flick overshoots, and the
micros needed to fix it do not fit before the tick.

Grip too loose and the flick arrives late, which the beat punishes just as fast.

<figure class="aim-figure">
<!-- aim:figure tension -->
<figcaption>One shot, five ways to spend it. Three of them land. Rush the correction and the
crosshair stops long; drag both halves out and the beat fires the shot while you are still
short.</figcaption>
</figure>

The tempo quietly teaches you **how much force a shot needs, and no more**. See
[Tension Management](../wiki/fundamentals/tension-management.md) for what those two failures feel
like away from a beat.

## Fade it out

**The metronome is scaffolding.** Leave it up permanently and you find out, in a game, that your timing
was borrowed.

One block, one scenario, a few sessions a week.

Take a week off it every month. If your unpaced pace moved, the tool did its job.

## What to run it with

Any metronome. A phone app, a browser tab, a click track.

Loud enough to hear over the game, no louder. Listening for the beat instead of the targets means the
volume is doing your aiming.
